Where a concentrate sale leaks money

A lot is paid at least twice. The first payment rests on the seller's own numbers. The final one rests on agreed assays, an averaged price and deductions, each reached on its own deadline.

01 — The first payment is only a down payment

The buyer typically pays about 90% on the seller's weights, assays and the metal price at shipment. The balance waits for the final invoice, weeks or months later, and that is where the arguments happen.

Sources: Thompson Creek–Glencore agreement · Gold Resource purchase contract

02 — The assay exchange runs on a clock

Each side assays its own sample and swaps results by a deadline. In one filed contract, a party without a certificate by the 60th day after the samples were sealed must accept the other side's number.

Source: concentrate sales agreement filed with the SEC

03 — A splitting limit is worth real money

Inside the limit, the two assays are averaged. Outside it, an umpire laboratory decides and the furthest party pays. On a 10,000 dmt parcel, a 0.25-point copper gap is about $331,000 at the 2026 average LME price of $13,235 a tonne. The parcel is illustrative; the limit and price are sourced.

Sources: Sterlite assay clause · Cochilco via Mining Reporters

04 — Payables and penalties move with every addendum

One Freeport-McMoRan contract raises the minimum copper deduction from 1.0 to 1.1 units when the grade falls below 24%. In Chile, 64.4% of contracts by tonnage carried an arsenic penalty in 2018–2020.

Sources: Freeport-McMoRan agreement · Cochilco

05 — 2026 rewrote the formulas

The benchmark treatment charge settled at $0 a tonne for 2026, down from $21.25. By July, Antofagasta was selling on spot-indexed terms with a floor. Every spreadsheet built around one annual number now has to change.

Sources: Guoxin Securities · Mining Reporters

About Valyria

The price is agreed once. The money is settled on paper.

A mine selling concentrate is paid on numbers it does not fully control: the weight and moisture a surveyor certifies, the assays two laboratories exchange, a monthly average of the LME price, and penalty scales for arsenic and other impurities. The contract sets the rules. Whether each invoice follows them is usually checked by hand.

2026 raised the stakes. The benchmark treatment charge settled at zero, spot charges went deeply negative, new contracts moved to index-linked formulas, and copper set records. Every hundredth of a percent on an assay certificate is worth more than it was.

Large traders run trading systems and operations teams for this. Valyria gives the commercial team of a mid-tier producer the same control, with the arithmetic done by deterministic code and every letter signed by a person.

Code does the maths

AI reads the documents. Deterministic code works out every number, and each line shows the clause and certificate behind it.

A person signs

Valyria drafts. Your team decides, signs and sends, and the log shows who did what and when.

We take no side

Why I'm building Valyria

I grew up around my family's industrial electrical distribution business. When I started working in it at seventeen, most of my time went to paper: invoices, price lists, spreadsheets and supplier letters. I learnt how much of a company's money is decided by documents nobody has time to read properly.

Concentrate settlement is the sharpest version of that problem I have found. The certificate is on file, the contract says exactly what it is worth, and the deadline is written into the clause. A mine can still give away a six-figure sum on one parcel because nobody put the three side by side in time.

Valyria does that for every lot. The arithmetic is deterministic, every line points to its clause and certificate, and a person on your team signs anything that leaves the building. If you run settlements for a mine or a trading book and think I have something wrong, I would like to hear it.

— Shriman
